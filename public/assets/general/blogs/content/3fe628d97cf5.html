In the era of Large Language Models, Building Prompts to make our LLMs stay focused on performing specific tasks and understand our use case is much needed and crucial. In this Blog, I would like to share about Prompt Tuning and Prompt Engineering which are cost-effective techniques we use in LLMs to attain performance over our task and business case.

Prompt Tuning and Prompt Engineering are techniques used to optimize the performance of large language models (LLMs). Prompt Tuning involves fine-tuning the prompts or inputs given to a pre-trained model, allowing it to perform specific tasks more effectively without extensive modifications to the model itself. This approach is efficient and leverages the model’s existing knowledge.

When I started learning about Prompting, I had a few questions about prompts,

  1. What is a Prompt?
  2. Why do I need to give a Prompt and how do these prompts affect the LLMs generation?
  3. Are there any rules & guidelines to create an effective prompt?
  4. Are there any limits and Cons for prompts?

We going to answer these few questions first and move on main topics,

What is a Prompt?

Prompts are nothing but a collection of tokens or sentences given by the user to an LLM, which will be injected along with user input every time, they are sets of instructions, scenarios, examples, and rules to follow given to a large language model (LLM) to guide its response generation. Similar to how a 6-year-old plays teacher-student play in her home, the child learns to mimic a teacher by observing classroom behaviors and recreating them at home, an LLM uses prompts to understand how to behave in specific situations. By providing clear instructions, we can make the LLM more effective for specific tasks, such as acting or behaving like a human and responding according to the scenario and use case with restrictions.

Why do I need to give a Prompt and how do these prompts affect the LLMs generation?

Remember that LLMs are models that predict what's next, the model would not have a clear direction on how to generate a relevant response and they don't have specific characteristics or behavior to perform a task unless it is Fine-tuned (specifically training the model) for that task. so we try to introduce the set of behaviors and characteristics through the prompts, for example: if you want your LLM to guide your customers for your retail shop to let the customers know about offers and discounts in a way that a storekeeper guy does, you can prompt as “ You are Store assistant in hardware retail store named as XY, where your objective is to guide customers to buy their desired products by letting them know offers and discount currently provided at XY hardware store, You have to respond politely to the customers.” These prompts will be injected along with the input so LLM can be aware of what it should and should not do while generating a response.

Are there any rules & guidelines to create an effective prompt?

Yes, there are a few things you must follow while prompting, Clearly state the task or behavior you want the LLM to perform and avoid ambiguity so a model can interpret your prompt. Provide relevant information and context to maintain the scenario, Use simple words, avoid complex sentences, and provide smaller steps to follow. You can also provide examples to make your LLM understand the assignment, these are called shots. There are three types of providing examples zero shot, one shot and a few shots, where you do not provide any examples is zero shot, providing one example is one shot and a few examples are few shots. Give a set of rules and limitations for the model to avoid, You can mention the tone ( kind, aggressive, polite ) and style that the LLM has to respond to. Also, ensure to provide how to handle when their input is out of context or model lacks information, or irrelevant input is given. Remember it's a repetitive process where keep updating and correcting your prompts to bring out an effective prompt for your model.

Are there any limits and Cons for prompts?

Of course, Huge or complex prompts may affect the performance and response time of the model. Efficiency can be impacted by the prompt’s length and structure. In General, LLMs have a maximum token limit for input and output. It differs from model to model, For example, models like GPT-4 have token limits (8192 tokens). This includes both the prompt combined input and the generated response. if your prompt is unclear or vague and does not contain enough relevant information can lead the LLM to hallucinate and provide irrelevant responses.

Let us discuss Prompt Tuning and Prompt Engineering and explore the differences between them

Prompt Engineering

Prompt Engineering is a method to guide language model’s predictions without altering their weights or modifying the parameters, it is a cost-effective method to make a single pre-trained model to perform different tasks without any task-specific fine-tuning. In General, an organization can’t train an LLM for each task repeatedly, prepare the dataset for each task-specific fine-tuning, and maintain multiple models at a time, which may result in high computational cost, time, and storage issues, To overcome this problem, we can have multiple prompts which are collection of token embedded with input, so the LLM could be aware its purpose, behavior and Do’s & Don’ts.

By providing a well-defined prompt, your LLM could perform a variety of tasks unless the prompts are vague, do not contain any ambiguity, and provide enough context to understand the task. This can be an iterative process where we keep testing the model with different prompts by updating the parameters in the prompt and checking the result.

import openai

openai.api_key = "your_api_key"

# Example of prompt engineering
prompt = """
Classify the sentiment of the following text as positive, negative, or neutral.

Text: "I love the new features of this product. It's amazing and very user-friendly."
Sentiment:
"""

response = openai.Completion.create(
    engine="text-davinci-003",
    prompt=prompt,
    max_tokens=10
)

print(response.choices[0].text.strip())

Here, the prompt is carefully constructed to instruct the model to classify the sentiment.

Prompt Tuning

Prompt tuning is a lightweight version of fine-tuning, where we modify the collection of additional parameters that are integrated into the model’s input processing stage. This method changes how the model recognizes input prompts without entirely modifying its weights, resulting in a balance of performance enhancement and resource efficiency. It is especially useful when the resources are limited or when versatility across multiple tasks is required because the approach maintains the original model weights unchanged.

Prompt tuning involves building specific prompt templates along with learning a small number of prompt parameters (often using a pre-trained model) to better guide the model’s outputs for specific tasks. It usually does not require considerable re-training of the model's basic parameters, instead, it focuses on improving how the input is given to the model.

import torch
from transformers import GPT2Tokenizer, GPT2LMHeadModel, Trainer, TrainingArguments
from datasets import load_dataset

# Load a pre-trained model and tokenizer
tokenizer = GPT2Tokenizer.from_pretrained('gpt2')
model = GPT2LMHeadModel.from_pretrained('gpt2')

# Load a dataset
dataset = load_dataset('imdb')

# Define a prompt tuning class
class PromptTuning(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, model, num_prompt_tokens):
        super().__init__()
        self.model = model
        self.num_prompt_tokens = num_prompt_tokens
        self.prompt_embeddings = torch.nn.Embedding(num_prompt_tokens, model.config.n_embd)

    def forward(self, input_ids, attention_mask=None):
        # Generate prompt ids
        prompt_ids = torch.arange(self.num_prompt_tokens, device=input_ids.device).unsqueeze(0).expand(input_ids.size(0), -1)
        prompt_embeddings = self.prompt_embeddings(prompt_ids)
        inputs_embeds = self.model.transformer.wte(input_ids)
        # Concatenate prompt embeddings with input embeddings
        inputs_embeds = torch.cat((prompt_embeddings, inputs_embeds), dim=1)
        attention_mask = torch.cat((torch.ones(prompt_embeddings.size()[:2], device=input_ids.device), attention_mask), dim=1)
        return self.model(inputs_embeds=inputs_embeds, attention_mask=attention_mask).logits

# Initialize prompt tuning model
num_prompt_tokens = 5
prompt_tuning_model = PromptTuning(model, num_prompt_tokens)

# Tokenize the dataset with a prompt template
def tokenize_function(examples):
    prompt_template = "Review: {} Sentiment: "
    return tokenizer([prompt_template.format(text) for text in examples['text']], padding='max_length', truncation=True, max_length=512)

tokenized_datasets = dataset.map(tokenize_function, batched=True)

# Training arguments
training_args = TrainingArguments(
    output_dir='./results',
    num_train_epochs=1,
    per_device_train_batch_size=4,
    per_device_eval_batch_size=4,
    logging_dir='./logs',
    logging_steps=10,
)

# Trainer setup
trainer = Trainer(
    model=prompt_tuning_model,
    args=training_args,
    train_dataset=tokenized_datasets['train'].shuffle().select(range(1000)),  # Use a subset for quick example
    eval_dataset=tokenized_datasets['test'].shuffle().select(range(100)),
)

# Train the model
trainer.train()

# Save the prompt embeddings
torch.save(prompt_tuning_model.prompt_embeddings.state_dict(), './prompt_embeddings.pth')

Conclusion

In this blog, we have seen about prompts and discussed what is Prompt engineering, Prompt tuning, and what it does. You can start with Prompt engineering at an early stage when you don’t want to change your model weights, see quick results, and experiment with the extent of your LLM’s capabilities. where you don’t need to provide effective prompts to guide a pre-trained model without training. Go for Prompt Tuning, when you need to adapt a pre-trained model to a specific task or domain without changing the model’s core parameters significantly and don’t have enough resources to fine-tune.
